A better score is not a lighter burden

For someone using a brain–computer interface, the cost that matters is not offline accuracy. It is how much of their day is spent recalibrating the decoder before it works. That quantity has a name, a formula, and a way of being gamed.

The curves on this page are synthetic. They are drawn from closed-form functions chosen to make the argument legible, not from any recording. Neural Accord does not treat simulated activity as evidence of BCI utility, and no calibration claim counts until it is run on permitted real data through a declared split, preprocessing, label budget, and baseline registry.

The quantity being measured

Bτ = min { n : P(n) ≥ τ }

n
Current-session labelled data — trials, prompted minutes, or target acquisitions.
P(n)
Task performance after calibrating with n.
τ
The usable-control threshold, declared before the run.
Bτ
The smallest n that reaches it. Lower is better. Undefined if the method never reaches τ.

Everything on this page follows from one property of that definition: Bτ depends on τ, and a method's ranking can invert as τ moves. That is not a flaw in the metric — it is the reason τ has to be fixed in advance, by the task's actual control requirement, rather than chosen after the curves are in.

What does not count as reducing burden

Not burden reduction

  • A higher final score reached only after more calibration than the baseline.
  • A threshold chosen after seeing the curves.
  • Improvement measured on a random split that lets future trials inform the past.
  • Gains that vanish once the decoder is run closed-loop instead of offline.
  • A win against a baseline that was not tuned, versioned, or run through the same pipeline.

Burden reduction

  • A lower Bτ at a predeclared τ, on permitted real data.
  • Fewer prompted trials or calibration minutes for the same closed-loop control.
  • Faster recovery to τ after drift, channel loss, or feature degradation.
  • The same gain reproduced across the declared seed list and reported with its variance.

Why the split has to be chronological

The relationship between neural features and intended movement shifts across minutes, days, and sessions. A random split lets a model learn from trials that had not happened yet, which quietly deletes the entire problem the track exists to study.

Chronological — the only permitted form
session 01 session 02 session 03 session 04 · evaluate
Random intermixing — not permitted
trials 01–04, shuffled train test leaks the future

Historical sessions supply the prior. Only the current session may supply labels, and only up to the declared budget.

There is no universal baseline

Every dataset and task profile declares its own baselines, versions them, and runs them through the same split, preprocessing, label budget, and reporting pipeline as the method under test. The Neural Accord method is never presumed superior — it is one row in its own registry.

What every BCI report must carry

The curve, not a point

  • Performance against current-session label budget
  • Area under the calibration curve
  • Zero-shot performance from historical sessions alone

Robustness

  • Recovery burden after drift, channel loss, or feature degradation
  • Retention cost — whether adapting to a new session damages prior sessions
  • Online adaptation and inference latency

The person's side

  • Prompted trials and calibration minutes
  • Failures and explicit recalibration events
  • Closed-loop metrics: target acquisition, throughput, path efficiency, time-to-target

Governance

  • Dataset access restrictions and the governing agreement
  • Preprocessing decisions, in order
  • Baseline versions and the split definition

Controlled human data are never redistributed outside their data-use agreement — including as derived features that would permit reconstruction. The repository holds manifests, checksums, transformations, and citations; the data stay where their agreement says they stay.
